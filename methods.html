Methods & evidence

How to check the work before you rely on it.

Aloha AI sells one thing above all others: outputs a regulator, a funder, or a court could not poke a hole in. That claim is worth nothing unless you can inspect it. This page is where you do that. It states where every figure comes from, what the tools can and cannot tell you, which data is live and which is a dated snapshot, and the claims this practice makes and the ones it deliberately does not.

What this practice claims, and what it does not

Most of the risk in this field comes from claims that outrun the evidence. The two columns below are the boundary. Everything else on the site is meant to stay inside it. If you find a page that steps outside, that is a defect. Tell me and I will correct it.

Claims I stand behind

  • Every figure I publish traces to a dated primary source you can open yourself.
  • A human stays accountable for every consequential output. Nothing here runs unsupervised.
  • The person who scopes your work is the person who builds it. There is no team behind the curtain.
  • The regulatory analysis is grounded in the actual statutes, rules, and agency actions, cited by number.
  • The trust and adoption methods draw on the research literature on how people decide what to believe.

Claims I do not make

  • This is not legal advice, and I am not a licensed attorney. I am a JD candidate.
  • The scored tools are not diagnostic or predictive instruments. They are structured self-reflection.
  • I do not claim clinical, actuarial, or scientific validity for any score the site produces.
  • I do not claim the dated monitors are live unless the page actually fetches current data.
  • I do not promise outcomes. I build the system and the evidence; the decision stays yours.

How every figure is sourced

The regulated-intelligence products and monitors are built on one discipline. Every fact that matters is recorded with its source, the date it was verified, and what a reader is entitled to conclude from it. I call the record a verification ledger. It is the same structure whether the fact sits in a product PDF, a monitor, or a client brief.

A single ledger entry looks like this:

Substance   Psilocybin
Fact   2026 DEA aggregate production quota = 50,000 g (up from 30,000 g in 2025)
Source   91 Fed. Reg. 287 (Jan. 5, 2026); FR Doc 2025-24277
Date verified   Q2 2026
Next-review trigger   annual APQ publication cycle (Q1)
What this supports   the authorized supply figure only; not a demand forecast or a market projection

The last line is the one that matters most. A source establishes a fact. It does not license every inference someone might want to draw from it. The ledger keeps those separate on purpose, because conflating them is how confident, well-sourced analysis still ends up wrong.

Which data is live, and which is a dated snapshot

Some pages fetch current primary-source data in your browser as they load. Others are analytical snapshots, accurate as of a stated date and labeled that way. The difference is not a matter of polish. It is whether a public, machine-readable source exists for that subject. Where one exists, the page is genuinely live. Where none exists, the page says "as of" and gives the date, rather than implying a freshness it cannot deliver.

The interactive tools are self-reflection, not diagnosis

The readiness scorecards, governance checkers, and assessments return a score and a tier. Read that number for what it is. It reflects the answers you gave, mapped against a structured rubric, to surface where attention is likely warranted. It is a conversation starter, not a measurement of a hidden trait.

None of these tools has been validated as a diagnostic or predictive instrument, and I do not present them as one. They do not diagnose your organization, predict an outcome, or substitute for review by a qualified professional in the relevant domain. Each tool states its own construct and limits at the point of use. If a score would carry real consequences for a decision, treat it as a prompt to look closer, not as the finding itself.

The legal boundary

Much of this work sits next to law: controlled-substances scheduling, platform policy, IP enforcement, professional-responsibility duties around AI. I read primary law carefully and cite it precisely. I am a JD candidate in administrative and regulatory law, not a licensed attorney. Nothing on this site is legal advice, creates an attorney–client relationship, or should be relied on in place of counsel admitted in your jurisdiction. What I provide is regulatory intelligence: the sourced, dated record you and your lawyer can both work from.

Corrections, dating, and versioning

Regulated facts change. A quota is revised, a rule is finalized, a case is decided. Two commitments follow from that. First, dated material carries its date, so you always know how fresh it is. Second, when something here is wrong, I fix it and, where it affected a published figure, I say what changed. This page itself is maintained. If you find an error, the fastest way to get it corrected is to send it to me directly.

This page was last reviewed in 2026. The claims and boundaries above govern every other page on the site. Where a specific figure appears elsewhere, its own source and date travel with it.

Why trust is treated as a research problem here

The through-line of this practice is that adoption of a tool has less to do with whether the tool is correct than with how a person receives it. That is not a slogan. It comes out of the research on source credibility, elaboration, and belief formation, and out of my own work in the psychology of how people process information under stakes. The design of the assessments, the way evidence is disclosed in layers, and the insistence on human accountability all follow from it. Selected background: Hovland & Weiss on source credibility (1951); Petty & Cacioppo's elaboration-likelihood work on how argument strength and processing route interact; and Elisha & Collins (2022), Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 9(1), 11–17, on within-group variation in how people absorb consequential information.
