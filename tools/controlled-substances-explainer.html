01The confusion is usually about structure, not facts+

Why this exists

A schedule label alone does not answer whether a proposed activity is permitted. The current federal rule, DEA registration requirements, FDA controls, institutional review, product status, and nonfederal law may each matter. This tool handles only the first routing step: it points configured substance names to dated federal source records and tells the user to verify the rest.

What it explains

For four configured substances, the tool returns a dated schedule reference, a current eCFR route, a Federal Register route, and a substance-specific verification instruction. It does not generate an explanation of the five schedules, agency jurisdiction, rescheduling procedure, or federal-state divergence.

What it will not do

It will not determine current legal status, permission, approval, registration, prescribing, research authorization, or the requirements applicable to an activity. It covers no unconfigured substance and no state, tribal, territorial, local, or international law.

02Choose a substance and a lens+

The explainer

Enter a question containing a configured substance name. The tool matches only its declared aliases and returns the corresponding dated federal routing record. If no configured alias matches, it abstains. It does not select a legal lens, assemble a legal analysis, or state current legality.

The routing match runs in your browser. Opening an eCFR or Federal Register link sends a normal request to that external site. No email signup is part of this tool.

03What the source routes mean—and do not mean+

Reference notes

These notes explain why the tool routes to sources instead of producing a legal answer. They are static documentation, not generated findings.

Five tiers, defined by criteria

The Controlled Substances Act and implementing regulations use five schedules with different statutory findings and controls. A schedule reference is not, by itself, an answer about a person's activity.

DEA, FDA, and the states

DEA administers and enforces the federal controlled-substances framework; FDA regulates drugs under separate authority; HHS has a statutory role in federal scheduling proceedings; states and other jurisdictions may impose separate rules. The router does not resolve those layers.

How status actually moves

Federal scheduling can change through administrative or legislative action. The existence of a proposal, hearing, recommendation, or docket does not itself establish that the controlling schedule has changed; verify the current rule and effective date.

Why federal and state disagree

A federal source route does not report or determine state, tribal, territorial, or local law. Those authorities require separate current research.

Where the current answer lives

Each matched record links to the relevant eCFR schedule section and a Federal Register route. The verification instruction identifies additional review that may be necessary; it is not a complete source list.

No claims that go stale

The tool abstains outside four configured substance aliases and avoids any nonfederal status claim. Absence of a match is not evidence that a substance is uncontrolled or an activity is permitted.

Do not skip this part

A useful result is still conditional on its evidence, method, assumptions, and limitations.

Evidence

  • User-supplied text containing a configured substance alias
  • Dated federal eCFR and Federal Register routes

Method

  1. Match only configured aliases
  2. Return the corresponding dated federal reference record
  3. Abstain when no configured substance matches
  4. Require current primary-source and qualified human verification

Assumptions

  • The user understands that the output is federal-only source routing, not an answer about an activity

Limitations

  • Covers only four configured substances and U.S. federal source routes
  • Does not determine legality, permission, approval, registration, prescribing, research, or state-law status
