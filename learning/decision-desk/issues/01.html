Should a small law firm use AI to summarize prospective-client intake?

A fluent synopsis can save minutes and still cost the firm the matter. Intake is the point at which unverified facts, prospective-client confidences, party names, limitations periods, emergencies, and a person’s expectations first enter the firm. The question is not whether a model can shorten the text. It is whether the firm can add a secondary representation without allowing it to become the record people actually trust.

Decision in one sentence: do not begin a live AI-intake pilot yet; redesign the intake first, evaluate a named product and configuration against representative test cases, and proceed only if the system, reviewers, data environment, and controlling jurisdiction pass every predetermined gate.

The scene before the software

A person usually arrives at intake with a story, not a data model. Names may be misspelled. A date may be buried in a paragraph. The person may not know which fact creates urgency. They may disclose information before the firm agrees to represent them. Under ABA Model Rule 1.18, even a person who does not become a client can be a prospective client, and information learned in the consultation can carry duties. ABA Formal Opinion 512 adds a separate warning for generative AI: lawyers must understand the tool well enough to protect information, supervise its use, communicate when required, and verify its work.12

That changes the design problem. The firm is not buying “summaries.” It is changing who or what gets the first opportunity to frame a legal story.

The jurisdiction changes the answer

California Rule 1.18(a)-(d), published in the State Bar's current court-adopted rules, supplies the controlling prospective-client, confidentiality, conflict, consent, limited-exposure, screening, and notice baseline. California's 2026 Practical Guidance remains official nonbinding guidance. Neither authority validates AI intake summarization.34

Colorado Rule 1.18 likewise protects prospective-client information and advises lawyers to limit the initial interview to information reasonably necessary for the representation decision. The court-hosted full compilation is historical, so the evidence package separately checks it against the Colorado Supreme Court's February 2025 rule-change order, which marks Rule 1.18 unchanged. No adopted Colorado intake-specific AI authority or product validation is claimed.35

There is no single nationwide intake-and-AI rule. Florida Opinion 24-1, for example, expressly discusses generative-AI chatbots used for client intake, including disclosure that the visitor is communicating with an AI program and lawyer responsibility for misleading or intrusive communications. It is still an advisory, nonbinding Florida opinion—not a national product approval. Arizona's ER 1.18(a)-(d), reproduced in a current official court guide, supplies its black-letter prospective-client, confidentiality, conflict, consent, and screening baseline; the guide is not itself the adoption order. Arizona's Supreme Court committee guidance separately addresses disclosure when prospective clients communicate with AI, but it remains nonbinding guidance. Michigan, Minnesota, Idaho, and Alaska reach the electronic-intake baseline through court-adopted Rule 1.18 texts, including commentary on invited electronic submissions and clear warnings; Minnesota, Idaho, and Alaska also counsel limiting initial collection. Alaska's rule expressly includes written, oral, and electronic communications, but no intake-specific Alaska AI authority or product validation is claimed. Michigan's actual State Bar AI FAQs supply general nonbinding guidance, while RI-392 concerns client files after representation and supports no AI proposition. Missouri's Rule 4-1.18 separately supplies its prospective-client baseline, and Informal Opinion 2024-11 addresses general AI-practice duties without validating intake summarization. Nebraska Supreme Court Rule § 3-501.18 supplies another controlling prospective-client baseline, including boundaries for unilateral communications and informed-consent conditions, but no intake-specific Nebraska AI authority is claimed. Montana uses different numbering: Rule 1.20—not Rule 1.18—supplies its controlling prospective-client baseline, including reasonable measures to limit exposure to disqualifying information. New Hampshire Supreme Court Rule 1.18 supplies the prospective-client baseline and conditions screening on reasonable limits during the initial consultation; no intake-specific New Hampshire AI authority is claimed. Mississippi remains open pending a current issuing-body text and pinpoint; open does not mean no authority exists. Arkansas's current rules index and a March 2026 disciplinary decision verify its Rule 1.18(b) confidentiality baseline; Arkansas Administrative Order 25 separately governs confidential or sealed court information disclosed to AI tools and cannot be converted into private-firm intake validation. Alabama's 2026 formal opinion supplies general AI-practice duties, while its State Bar intake handbook recommends limited initial collection and conflict screening; neither source supports claiming an adopted Rule 1.18 analogue. Georgia, by contrast, has a current Rule 1.18 baseline and a July 2026 State Bar toolkit that expressly addresses chatbots and virtual assistants used for prospective-client intake. Louisiana, Maine, and Maryland each now have a verified controlling prospective-client baseline. Maine and Maryland expressly counsel limiting the initial interview; Maryland's June 2026 AI language remains committee-stage proposal material, not controlling law. None of these rules validates AI summarization. Kentucky E-457 supplies formally adopted general AI-practice guidance, but it does not decide advertising questions or validate intake summarization.

This authority hierarchy must remain visible in the final tool: controlling jurisdictional rules first; state or local ethics opinions at their actual legal weight; national model guidance and technical standards as persuasive or voluntary sources; and product-specific evidence as a separate empirical question.

System X-Ray: where a synopsis can distort the work

  1. Submission: the person supplies names, dates, allegations, documents, contact details, and expectations.
  2. Preservation: the original becomes the authoritative record; access and retention rules attach.
  3. Conflict screen: structured party data is checked. A generated synopsis must never be the sole input.
  4. Urgency and issue screen: a trained human looks for deadlines, safety concerns, jurisdiction, and missing facts.
  5. Routing decision: the firm schedules, seeks more information, declines, or refers without implying representation.
  6. Communication: the person receives an accurate next step and knows what the firm has—and has not—agreed to do.

The model belongs, if anywhere, between preservation and human review. It produces a labeled aid. It does not clear conflicts, calculate deadlines, accept or reject matters, decide legal merit, or replace the source record.

RN’s finding

Redesign and evaluate before any live pilot.

The current evidence supports duties, safeguards, and evaluation requirements. It does not establish that a particular AI system can summarize prospective-client intake accurately, equitably, or usefully in a real firm. No directly applicable primary study was located in this research pass. That is an evidence boundary—not proof that no such study exists.

The sequence matters. A firm should first make the human workflow legible: preserve the original, collect conflict and urgency fields independently, identify the controlling jurisdictions, and assign responsibility. It may then run a pre-deployment shadow evaluation of a named product and configuration on representative fictional, synthetic, or appropriately governed historical cases. A live pilot is a later decision, not the starting point.

What the empirical evidence can—and cannot—tell us

Adjacent primary research makes three cautions visible. A 2024 comparative study of patent-document summarization identified incompleteness and hallucination as separate error modes; a single aggregate quality score can conceal the difference. A 2026 legal-deposition research prototype used source-linked “fact nuggets” to expose missing, unsupported, or inaccurate content, but it tested nine public transcripts and did not report a controlled operational-effectiveness trial. NIST’s 2026 monitoring report separates functionality, operations, human factors, security, compliance, and large-scale effects, while acknowledging that validated post-deployment methods remain nascent.567

None of those sources supplies an intake accuracy rate. None validates a vendor. None shows that a reviewer will reliably resist a fluent but incomplete synopsis. Their proper use is to shape the evaluation—not to manufacture confidence.

“A human checks it” is a testable claim, not a control

Two controlled studies in adjacent clinical settings make the human-review problem concrete. Participants in a 2024 AI decision-support experiment followed erroneous AI recommendations under some conditions; a 2023 mammography experiment found that incorrect AI suggestions adversely affected readers across experience levels in that study. Neither experiment involved lawyers, intake forms, or generated legal summaries. They therefore cannot predict how often a legal reviewer will miss an intake error. They do show why the firm cannot count the presence of a person as evidence that review works.89

The pre-deployment evaluation must observe behavior: seed known omissions and unsupported additions, measure whether reviewers open and compare the original, record correction and escalation, and test whether time pressure or interface design changes performance. If reviewers repeatedly treat the synopsis as the record, the workflow fails even when the model's aggregate score appears acceptable.

Accessibility begins before the summary

An AI synopsis cannot repair information that an inaccessible intake form prevented someone from submitting. U.S. Department of Justice guidance identifies unlabeled fields, unclear instructions, inaccessible error messages, and mouse-only navigation as barriers; it also cautions that an automated checker alone cannot establish accessibility. The intake form, upload path, confirmation, reviewer interface, and later communication should be tested manually with keyboard navigation, screen-reader output, zoom and reflow, contrast, error recovery, and people using relevant assistive technologies.10

That federal guidance is an official baseline, not a complete legal opinion for every firm. The firm still must determine which federal, state, and local requirements apply to its offices, services, and delivery model.

Decision sequence

  1. Redesign: document the non-AI workflow, authoritative record, structured fields, owners, and escalation paths.
  2. Specify: name the product, model, configuration, integrations, matter class, users, and jurisdictions.
  3. Govern: complete ethics, contract, privacy, security, accessibility, retention, and data-flow review.
  4. Evaluate: use representative test cases and a fact-level rubric for omissions, distortions, unsupported additions, urgency signals, and routing consequences.
  5. Observe humans: test whether reviewers actually compare source and synopsis and whether the tool changes judgment, rework, or delay.
  6. Decide: authorize a bounded live pilot only after every gate passes; otherwise redesign, reject the product, or keep intake human-led.

Pre-deployment evaluation conditions

1. Define the boundary

  • One office, one approved system, one or two low-complexity matter categories, and a fixed test period.
  • No autonomous conflict clearance, deadline calculation, legal assessment, engagement, declination, referral, or outbound advice.
  • No use of the synopsis outside intake without a separately documented purpose.

2. Control the information

  • Document what the vendor receives, retains, logs, trains on, exposes to subcontractors, and deletes.
  • Minimize free text where a structured field is safer and more reliable.
  • Preserve the original submission and an audit trail tying each synopsis to its source and model/configuration version.

3. Design human review

  • The reviewer compares source and synopsis—not synopsis alone.
  • The screen explicitly checks parties, dates, requested relief, urgency, jurisdiction, contradictory facts, and missing attachments.
  • Overrides and corrections are captured as evidence about the system, not treated as invisible cleanup.

4. Test access and comprehension

  • Test the original form, attachments, confirmation, synopsis, and follow-up—not only the public landing page.
  • Combine automated checks with manual keyboard, screen-reader, zoom/reflow, contrast, label, instruction, and error-recovery testing.
  • Include representative disabled users and language needs in the test design; document exclusions rather than treating them as silent edge cases.

5. Communicate accurately

  • The intake experience must not imply that an attorney has reviewed the submission when none has.
  • Any automation disclosure should be understandable, placed where it matters, and consistent with the actual workflow.
  • Engagement and non-engagement communications remain human-approved.

Evaluation gates and stop rules

MeasureEvidenceStop condition
Material fidelityHuman comparison against original submissionsAny invented party, date, allegation, or requested action
Urgency detectionKnown-signal test set plus live reviewAny missed deadline, safety, or emergency signal
ConfidentialityAccess, retention, and incident logsAny unauthorized disclosure or unapproved reuse
Reviewer behaviorObservation and exception logRepeated reliance on synopsis without source comparison
AccessibilityAutomated and manual checks plus representative assistive-technology useAny blocker to submission, correction, confirmation, source review, or follow-up
Operational valueCycle time and rework, measured togetherFaster first pass but higher corrections, repeat contacts, or routing errors

The reusable tool: Intake Summarization Evaluation Card

Subscribers receive a completed example and a blank version. The card separates the pre-deployment decision from any later live pilot. It records: business purpose; jurisdictions; in-scope matters; prohibited uses; named system and configuration; data lifecycle; authoritative record; representative test set; reviewer protocol; fact-level error taxonomy; accessibility and language checks; human-factors observations; gate thresholds; stop authority; incident path; review date; and final disposition. The disposition is one of four outcomes: reject, redesign and retest, continue human-led, or authorize a separately governed bounded pilot.

Further reading and learning

  • Read the firm’s jurisdiction-specific professional-conduct rules and ethics opinions; ABA Model Rules are not themselves the controlling law everywhere.
  • Map the existing intake process before evaluating a tool. If the source workflow has no authoritative record or accountable reviewer, fix that first.
  • Use the NIST profile to expand the pilot card into a fuller risk register when the use case grows.
