# Public copy and plain-language standard

Status: governing standard for all public-facing Decision Desk masterclass and clinic copy  
Audience baseline: Grade 10 reading level or easier, without removing necessary legal, ethical, or technical meaning

## Core rule

Meet people at the problem they already recognize. A visitor should understand the title, who the session is for, what they will do, what they will leave with, and what the session will not do without first learning Aloha AI's internal vocabulary.

Public copy must lead with the visitor's question, task, risk, or desired outcome. Internal framework and instrument names may appear after the plain-language explanation, not in place of it.

## Two-layer naming system

Each offer has two names:

1. **Public title:** a short question or outcome in everyday language.
2. **Internal tool name:** the precise map, test, gate, matrix, dossier, or decision record used in research, teaching, and governance.

“Decision Desk” may identify the overall series. It must not carry the burden of explaining an individual offer.

## Acceptance tests

Public copy passes only when:

- the title is understandable without reading a subtitle;
- the first sentence states the real-world situation and audience benefit;
- sentences usually stay under 25 words;
- paragraphs usually contain no more than three sentences;
- common words replace specialist terms when meaning is preserved;
- every necessary specialist term is explained the first time it appears;
- headings describe a question, action, result, or boundary;
- calls to action say what happens next;
- abstract nouns and stacked qualifiers are reduced;
- active voice is preferred;
- “you” is used when it makes the action clearer;
- reading-level software reports Grade 10 or easier for the visitor-facing body copy, with manual review after scoring; and
- a plain-language review confirms that safety language is clear, not merely shorter.

A numerical readability score is evidence, not the final judge. Proper names, legal terms, and short bullet fragments can distort a score.

## Do not simplify away

Plain language must not remove:

- who has authority to decide;
- what evidence is required;
- privacy, confidentiality, consent, and access limits;
- legal or jurisdiction-specific uncertainty;
- human review and appeal or correction paths;
- stop conditions;
- exclusions and non-advice boundaries; or
- the difference between a draft, test, recommendation, approval, and live offer.

If a required boundary is complex, split it into shorter statements and add an example. Do not hide it in a dense disclaimer.

## Required review surfaces

Apply this standard independently to:

- program and monthly titles;
- subtitles and page descriptions;
- navigation labels;
- hero copy;
- audience and outcome statements;
- session agendas;
- form labels and instructions;
- calls to action;
- eligibility, exclusion, privacy, refund, and support copy;
- email subject lines and message bodies;
- workbooks, slides, facilitator scripts, and follow-up resources; and
- error, decline, correction, and incident messages.

Internal research files may retain technical language, but any text copied into a visitor or participant surface must pass this standard first.

## Comprehension check

Before release, ask at least three people who match the intended audience to answer:

1. What is this session about?
2. Is it for you?
3. What will you do?
4. What will you leave with?
5. What will not happen?
6. What would you click or do next?

Do not claim this check occurred until real people complete it. Confusion about any answer is a copy defect to fix and retest.
