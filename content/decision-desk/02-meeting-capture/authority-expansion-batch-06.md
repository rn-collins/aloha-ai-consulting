## Massachusetts — verified recording layer

### Controlling current text

1. Massachusetts General Court, G.L. c. 272, § 99:  
   https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartIV/TitleI/Chapter272/Section99

Issuer: Massachusetts General Court. Retrieval and verification date: 2026-08-08.

### Pinpoint propositions

- Section 99(B)(4) defines “interception” as secretly hearing, secretly recording, or helping another secretly hear or record a wire or oral communication through a device by a person other than one given prior authority by all parties.
- Section 99(C)(1) prohibits willful interception, attempted interception, and procurement of another person to intercept.
- Section 99(C)(3) separately prohibits willful disclosure or use when the person knows the information came from an interception.
- Section 99(D) contains narrow carrier, office-intercommunication, law-enforcement, warrant, safety, and specified financial-institution exceptions.
- Those exceptions do not create general permission for an ordinary workplace or commercial meeting bot.
- Because secrecy is part of the statutory definition, a separate review must determine what notice, knowledge, and prior authority mean in the exact setting; this record does not treat a platform indicator, passive presence, or silence as proven authority.

### Bounded claim

Within Massachusetts’s recording layer, a person avoids the statutory definition of secret interception only when all parties gave prior authority, or when a specific statutory exception applies. This is not a conclusion that any particular notice or consent flow is valid, accessible, informed, or noncoercive.

Disposition remains `human_notes_only`. No meeting bot, recording, transcription, or product test is authorized.

Issuer: Michigan Legislature. Retrieval date: 2026-08-08. The official chapter and target sections were identified, and the official index exposed the relevant section structure; full current text was not reliably retrievable through the research interface in this pass.

### What the official materials support

- The official chapter identifies MCL 750.539c as “Eavesdropping upon private conversation” and MCL 750.539d as device use for observing, recording, transmitting, photographing, or eavesdropping in a private place.
- The official chapter reproduction states that “eavesdrop” or “eavesdropping” means to overhear, record, amplify, or transmit part of the private discourse of others without all parties’ permission.
- That wording creates a material participant boundary: the text addresses the private discourse “of others,” while the all-parties clause applies inside the definition.
- A safe operational rule cannot be selected from the statutory words alone without reconciling current controlling Michigan construction, including whether and when a participating recorder falls outside “of others.”
- Until that work is complete, this record does not call Michigan either a one-party or all-party jurisdiction for meeting capture.

### Bounded claim

Michigan’s official statutory framework supports an all-parties rule for conduct that meets its definition of eavesdropping on the private discourse of others. The participant-recorder question remains unresolved for this project, so the conservative operational default is documented consent from every participant, not a claim that Michigan law always requires it.

Disposition remains `human_notes_only`. No meeting bot, recording, transcription, or product test is authorized.

## Minnesota — verified recording layer

### Controlling current text

1. Minnesota Revisor of Statutes, Minn. Stat. § 626A.02:  
   https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/626a.02
2. Official Minnesota Statutes 2025, Chapter 626A PDF:  
   https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/626A/pdf
3. Minnesota Revisor of Statutes, Chapter 626A:  
   https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/626A/full

Issuer: Minnesota Revisor of Statutes. The official publication is Minnesota Statutes 2025. Retrieval and verification date: 2026-08-08.

### Pinpoint propositions

- Section 626A.02, subdivision 1, prohibits intentional interception and specified knowing use or disclosure of unlawfully intercepted wire, electronic, or oral communications.
- Section 626A.02, subdivision 2(d), provides that a private person does not violate the chapter when that person is a party or one party gave prior consent.
- The private-person route does not apply when the communication is intercepted to commit a criminal or tortious act under federal or state law.
- Subdivision 4 supplies criminal consequences, and subdivision 5 supplies a civil-suit route subject to its exact terms.
- Section 626A.04 separately restricts evidentiary use when disclosure would violate the chapter.
- The chapter’s provider, public-access, radio, interference, and law-enforcement provisions do not establish general permission for an ordinary meeting bot.

### Bounded claim

Within Minnesota’s recording layer, a private person has a statutory route when the recorder is a party or one party gave prior consent, unless the prohibited-purpose limit applies. This does not resolve multistate law, privilege, employment power, vendor processing, retention, generated summaries, or later disclosure.

Disposition remains `human_notes_only`. No meeting bot, recording, transcription, or product test is authorized.
