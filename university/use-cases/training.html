01What this use case is+

The idea

AI for training turns raw material you already have — SOPs, recordings, a subject expert's brain dump — into structured learning: a module outline, a script, practice scenarios, knowledge checks, and a quick-reference guide. It will also play the other side of a role-play, so a new rep can rehearse a hard customer call ten times before doing it live.

It does not replace a trainer. It replaces the blank page and the weeks of formatting that keep training from ever getting built.

02Who this is for+

Who it helps

Small teams with no L&D function

Where "training" means shadowing whoever happens to be free that day.

Managers onboarding new hires

Who want a consistent path instead of a different experience every time.

Subject-matter experts

Who know the work cold but cannot turn it into a teachable module.

Customer-facing teams

Who need safe reps at hard conversations before the real one.

03The business problem it solves+

The problem

Training rarely fails because people cannot teach. It fails because building it is slow, so it never happens — and new hires learn by absorbing whatever is nearby, correct or not. Inconsistent onboarding shows up months later as inconsistent quality and preventable mistakes.

This workflow collapses the build time. An expert talks for thirty minutes, and the model turns that into a usable module by the afternoon. Role-play practice moves the awkward first attempts off your real customers and onto a simulator, so people arrive at the live conversation already warmed up.

04What you need going in+

Inputs

Source material

An existing SOP, a recorded walkthrough, or a transcript of an expert explaining the task out loud.

The learner and the goal

Who is being trained, and what they should be able to do at the end.

Real scenarios

Especially the hard and common ones: the objection you always get, the mistake people always make.

Your standard of "correct"

So the model builds toward how your business actually does the work.

05Suggested tools+

Tools

ChatGPT or Claude

Turn a transcript into an outline, script, quiz, and role-play partner. Voice modes let learners practice a live conversation out loud.

NotebookLM

Load your SOPs and generate a study guide, FAQ, or audio overview grounded strictly in your own procedures.

Gamma or Canva

Take the AI-written outline and turn it into slides or a one-page job aid without design work.

On accuracy: a model will happily invent a plausible-sounding procedure. Ground it in your real SOP and have the expert review the draft, or you will teach a confident version of the wrong thing.
06Step by step+

The workflow

  1. Record the expert. Have your best person talk through the task for 20 to 30 minutes, then transcribe it.
  2. Generate the outline. Ask the model to structure the transcript into modules with clear learning objectives.
  3. Draft the content — the script, examples, and a one-page quick-reference — from that outline.
  4. Build knowledge checks: a short quiz that tests doing, not just remembering.
  5. Create role-play scenarios for the hard conversations, with the model playing the customer or the difficult case.
  6. Run expert review. The subject expert corrects anything the model got wrong or oversimplified.
  7. Ship and iterate. Run it with a real new hire, then fix the parts that confused them.
07An example prompt+

The prompt

Use this to turn an expert's brain dump into a training module:

Below is a transcript of our most experienced technician explaining how to handle a warranty claim. Build a training module for a new hire in their first week. Produce, in order: 1. Three learning objectives, each starting with "By the end, you will be able to..." 2. A step-by-step walkthrough in plain language, calling out the two mistakes new people most often make. 3. A five-question knowledge check that tests judgment, not memorization. Include an answer key with brief explanations. 4. One realistic role-play: you play a frustrated customer whose claim is borderline. Stay in character until I resolve it. Use only what is in the transcript. If a step is unclear or missing, list it as an open question for our expert instead of guessing. Transcript: [paste]

A good response comes back usable the same day: three objectives, a walkthrough flagging the two common mistakes, a graded quiz, and an in-character role-play. Its most honest move is the open question — where it tells you the transcript never covered lost-receipt claims, it is pointing straight at a hole in your source material to fill.

08What a human still owns+

Guardrails

Expert sign-off is mandatory

Someone who does the job for real must confirm every procedure before it trains anyone.

Kill invented steps

If a step was not in your source and the expert did not add it, cut it. Do not let the model backfill.

Check the quiz answers

Models write plausible questions with subtly wrong answer keys. Verify each one.

Keep it current

When the real process changes, the module changes the same day. Assign an owner.

This is not legal, medical, or financial advice. For safety-critical, regulated, or compliance training, a qualified human designs and approves the content. AI drafts, people certify.

Use it responsibly

What supports this—and where it stops.

Keep the practical material attached to its evidence, method, assumptions, and limits.

Evidence

  • Approved SOPs and source materials
  • Defined learning objectives
  • Expert review records
  • Pilot learner performance and feedback

Method

  1. Ground instruction in canonical sources
  2. Align practice with objectives
  3. Separate generation from expert approval
  4. Test accessibility and ambiguity
  5. Revise from observed learning

Assumptions

  • Subject-matter experts validate content
  • Learner data is handled appropriately

Limitations

  • AI can teach errors from incomplete sources
  • Generated assessments may be biased, ambiguous, or poor measures of competence
