Before you automate the task

Leaders don't need AI to write their vision. They need it to compress the noise — the board deck, the six status reports, the messy off-site notes — so their scarce attention goes to the one call only they can make.

This use case is a practical pattern, not permission to move sensitive data, delegate professional judgment, or let an AI system act without the review and authority the real task requires.

01What this use case is+

The idea

AI for leadership is using a language model as a thinking partner and a synthesis engine — never as a decision-maker. It reads the long inputs a leader can't personally get through, surfaces the tensions and the missing pieces, drafts the communication, and stress-tests the plan by arguing the other side. The judgment stays with the human. The reading, summarizing, and first-drafting moves to the machine.

Done well, this is not about sounding smart faster. It is about walking into a decision having genuinely considered the case against it.

02Who this is for+

Who it helps

Founders and owner-operators

Who are the strategy function, the HR function, and the sales function all at once.

Department heads

Preparing board updates, all-hands messages, and reorg rationales under time pressure.

New managers

Who need to give feedback, run 1:1s, and write decisions clearly before the instinct is built.

Anyone facing a hard call

Who wants a rigorous devil's advocate on demand instead of a room of nodding heads.

03The business problem it solves+

The problem

Leadership fails quietly in two places: decisions made without hearing the strongest counterargument, and communication that lands vaguely because it was written in a hurry. Both come from the same shortage — time to think and time to draft.

This workflow buys back both. It turns a leader's raw thinking into a clean memo in minutes, and it forces a structured pre-mortem before a big commitment, so the failure modes surface while they're still cheap to avoid. You still decide. You just decide with better inputs, and you communicate the decision so people actually understand it.

04What you need going in+

Inputs

The raw material of the decision

The reports, the data points, the constraints, the context other people handed you.

Your actual position

In whatever rough form. The model works better refining your view than inventing one.

Who the message is for

A board, a frontline team, and a nervous client each need a different draft.

Your non-negotiables

The values, the tone, and the lines you will not cross — stated up front.

05Suggested tools+

Tools

Claude or ChatGPT

The core thinking partner. Best for synthesis, pre-mortems, and drafting communication in your voice. Use a business tier for anything confidential.

NotebookLM

Load a stack of reports and quarterly data, then ask it to summarize the tensions and open questions with citations back to each source.

Otter or your meeting-notes tool

Capture the off-site or the leadership discussion as text, so the model has the real raw material to work from.

Confidentiality note: board matters, personnel decisions, and financials are among the most sensitive inputs a business has. Use a paid business or enterprise tier with training turned off, and strip the names and numbers you don't need the model to see.
06Step by step+

The workflow

  1. Dump the inputs. Paste the reports, notes, and constraints into one conversation without polishing them.
  2. Ask for synthesis first. Have the model summarize the situation, the tensions, and what's missing — before you ask for any recommendation.
  3. State your leaning and ask the model to argue the strongest case against it. Read that case honestly.
  4. Run a pre-mortem. Assume the decision failed a year from now, and ask the model to reconstruct why.
  5. Decide. This step is yours alone. Write your call and your reasoning in a sentence.
  6. Draft the communication for each audience, then edit until it sounds like you, not like a model.
  7. Sanity-check for tone and accuracy before anything reaches a real inbox.
07An example prompt+

The prompt

A pre-mortem prompt you can reuse for any significant decision:

I'm leaning toward [decision: e.g., consolidating our two support teams into one and cutting the vendor contract]. Here is my reasoning and the context: [paste]. Do three things, in order: 1. Summarize the strongest case FOR this decision in three bullets. 2. Argue the strongest case AGAINST it as if you were a smart skeptic on my team. Do not soften it. 3. Run a pre-mortem: it's 12 months later and this decision clearly failed. Give the three most likely reasons why, and one early warning sign for each. Do not tell me what to do. I'll decide.

What good looks like back: no recommendation, but the coverage gap and the retention risk laid out plainly — the overnight tickets nobody backfilled, the two strong performers who quit six months in, each with an early warning sign you can actually watch for. That's the point. You walk into the decision having seen the risks before committing, not after.

08What a human still owns+

Guardrails

The decision is never the model's

Use it to see more clearly, then own the call and its consequences yourself.

Check the facts it asserts

A model will state a market figure or a legal consequence confidently and wrongly. Verify anything load-bearing.

Edit for voice

A message that sounds generic reads as a leader who didn't care. Rewrite until it's unmistakably you.

Guard sensitive judgment

Personnel, compensation, and legal-exposure calls need a human — and often a real professional — not a chatbot.

This is not legal, medical, or financial advice. AI can sharpen how you think about a decision; it cannot be accountable for it. That accountability is the job.

Use it responsibly

What supports this—and where it stops.

Keep the practical material attached to its evidence, method, assumptions, and limits.

Evidence

  • Decision record
  • Source documents and metrics
  • Stakeholder constraints
  • Human rationale and approval

Method

  1. Use AI to widen and compress
  2. Separate facts from assumptions
  3. Request counterarguments
  4. Preserve accountability
  5. Track outcomes against the decision

Assumptions

  • Leaders can identify authoritative evidence
  • AI supports rather than makes consequential decisions

Limitations

  • Models can reinforce framing bias or invent certainty
  • Leadership judgment and stakeholder responsibility cannot be delegated
