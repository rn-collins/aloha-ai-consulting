Before you choose a tool

Some tools answer with citations. Some read forty papers so you do not have to. Some just sound authoritative. Here is how to tell them apart — and match the tool to the kind of research you are actually doing.

"Research" covers very different jobs. Checking a current fact with a source is not the same as running a literature review, and neither is the same as reasoning through a messy strategic question. The right tool depends on which of those you mean. This guide sorts by the job, flags where each tool cites honestly versus where it can fabricate, and gives you a check for verifying what any of them return.

02Strengths and limitations+

The honest trade

What they do well

  • Citation-first tools let you verify claims at the source instead of trusting a black box — the single most important property in research.
  • Structured extraction turns a stack of PDFs into a comparable table in minutes, work that used to take days.
  • Deep-research modes are genuinely good at breadth: pulling many angles into one readable draft you then refine.
  • Live-web tools stay current, which matters for anything that changes month to month — regulations, pricing, competitors.

Where they fall short

  • Every one of these tools can misread a source or fabricate a citation that looks real. Open the link. If a paper or quote cannot be found, treat it as false.
  • Citations prove a source exists, not that the tool summarized it correctly. Skim the original for anything load-bearing.
  • Deep-research reports read as authoritative regardless of quality. Confidence in the output is not evidence.
  • Academic tools cover indexed literature; they miss paywalled, very recent, or non-English work relevant to your question.
  • None replace domain expertise. They accelerate a knowledgeable researcher and mislead an unknowledgeable one.
03Privacy considerations+

Read before you upload

Research often means uploading documents you do not own or should not share — draft manuscripts, licensed PDFs, client materials. A few guardrails:

  • Check training use. Free and consumer tiers may use your uploads to improve the product. Paid and enterprise tiers usually do not, but confirm the setting on the vendor's page.
  • Mind file retention. Some tools keep uploads for a set window. Know the retention period before you upload anything confidential, and delete when done.
  • Respect licensing. Uploading a paywalled paper to a third-party tool may breach the publisher's terms even if the tool itself is trustworthy — a compliance question, not just a privacy one.
  • Keep client work separate. For anything covered by confidentiality, use a tier with a signed data agreement and training off — or do not upload it at all.
04Pricing considerations+

What it costs

  • Perplexity offers free, individual paid, and organizational tiers with different research limits. Confirm current limits and terms on the vendor page.
  • Elicit offers a paid research tier for sustained literature work. Confirm current features and billing terms on the vendor page.
  • Consensus offers a free tier with paid plans for heavier use; check current numbers on the vendor page.
  • Deep-research modes are available on several paid assistant plans, with query allowances varying by tier. Confirm current limits before relying on them.

For most people, one citation-first web tool plus a general assistant covers the majority of research needs. Add a specialist academic tool only if you regularly run literature reviews.

05Workflow fit+

How to sequence it

A reliable research loop, regardless of tool:

  • Scope the question tightly with a general assistant — vague questions produce vague research.
  • Gather with a citation-first tool (Perplexity for the web, Elicit or Consensus for academic questions).
  • Verify every load-bearing claim at its source. This step is non-negotiable and is where the tools quietly fail.
  • Synthesize your verified findings with an assistant, keeping your own note of which claim came from where.

The tools change the gather step from days to minutes. The verify step still belongs to you.

Use it responsibly

What supports this—and where it stops.

Keep the practical material attached to its evidence, method, assumptions, and limits.

Evidence

  • Primary sources retrieved independently
  • Representative search and synthesis tests
  • Current product documentation
  • Citation-verification records

Method

  1. Separate discovery from authority
  2. Prefer primary sources
  3. Test citation existence and entailment
  4. Record search boundaries
  5. Preserve uncertainty and contradictory evidence

Assumptions

  • The researcher can access and inspect authoritative sources
  • Consequential conclusions receive qualified review

Limitations

  • Search coverage is incomplete and model-dependent
  • Citations may be wrong, stale, or unsupported
