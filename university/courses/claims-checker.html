01A claim you can't back up is a bill waiting to arrive+

Why this, why now

Regulators are active right now. The FTC opens cases over unsubstantiated health, performance, and green claims; the FDA sends warning letters over disease and structure-function language; state AGs and platforms pull listings and ads that overstate. A single line of copy that promises more than your evidence supports can trigger a penalty, a warning letter, or a takedown.

The hard part is not knowing rules exist. It is catching the specific claim, mapping it to the specific rule, and knowing what proof it needs, before the copy goes live. That is the gap this course teaches you to close, with a tool you build and own.

02A checker that reads your copy the way a regulator would+

What you'll build

The open materials guide you through a bounded prototype that turns a piece of copy into inspectable claim records, candidate governing questions, evidence gaps, provisional dispositions, and named human-review requirements. It does not determine applicable law, verify substantiation, or approve publication.

Every claim in the copy

Pulls each express and implied claim, not just the obvious headline.

Claim to the rule

Matches each claim to the applicable regime: FTC substantiation, FDA, state, industry code.

Unsubstantiated, overstated, prohibited

A verdict per claim with the reason and the risk level.

The substantiation needed

Tells you what proof each claim needs before it can run, or that it cannot run at all.

A review-ready audit

Verdicts, rules, and required proof, ready to hand to legal or a client.

Human signs off

On doubt it defaults to flag for review. You stay accountable; the tool assists.

03Nine modules, each producing part of the evidence packet+

Curriculum

Each module includes a practice record that contributes to the bounded capstone: scope, extraction, authority, mapping, disposition, governance, evaluation, or release evidence.

  1. The problem and the standard. How claims get regulated, what substantiation means, and the difference between express and implied claims. You write your claim-risk rubric.
  2. Set up your workspace. Accounts, model access, project structure, with zero assumptions and nothing technical taken for granted.
  3. Extract the claims. Build the step that pulls every claim from a piece of copy, including the implied ones a reader would hear.
  4. Build your rule library. Load the regimes that apply to your industry: FTC substantiation, FDA language limits, state rules, platform and industry codes.
  5. Map claim to rule, the core. Match each claim to the rule that governs it and classify the required substantiation. This is the evidence-grounded heart of the tool.
  6. Verdicts and report. Substantiated, needs-proof, overstated, prohibited, or can't-assess, with the rule cited and the proof required.
  7. Governance layer. Guardrails, uncertainty handling, default-to-human, and a full audit log of every screen.
  8. Evaluate your checker. Build a test set of real and borderline claims; measure catch rate and false positives; iterate.
  9. Package the bounded prototype and its evidence. Use synthetic or approved public examples until authority, confidentiality, qualified review, and organizational release controls are resolved.

02Bound the product, audience, and jurisdiction+

  1. Resolve product, audience, channel, and jurisdiction

    45 minutes

    The same sentence can face different rules when used for a drug, supplement, cosmetic, food, device, service, or general consumer product. Audience and channel also change the surrounding impression.

    Open lesson →

  2. Create the claim-review record

    45 minutes

    A reproducible review unit preserves the source creative, exact claim, surrounding context, audience, product class, jurisdiction, authority version, evidence supplied, machine suggestion, human disposition, limitations, owner, and timestamps.

03Extract express and implied claims+

  1. Extract express claims without losing context

    55 minutes

    Start with deterministic segmentation of headings, body copy, captions, calls to action, labels, footnotes, and visible qualifications. Preserve the exact span and its location.

    Open lesson →

  2. Test the net impression for implied claims

    60 minutes

    An implied claim may arise from words, images, demonstrations, omissions, sequence, or the relationship among statements. The unit of review is the communication's net impression, not isolated grammar.

    Open lesson →

04Build the authority register+

  1. Rank authorities by question and date

    50 minutes

    Statutes, regulations, binding orders, official labeling rules, and agency guidance do not carry identical legal force. Record authority type, jurisdiction, effective date, scope, and the exact question it answers.

    Open lesson →

  2. Turn authority into inspectable rule records

    55 minutes

    Each rule record should identify the source, quoted or accurately paraphrased requirement, covered claim class, prerequisites, exceptions, required qualification, evidence expectation, reviewer, and version.

    Open lesson →

05Map claims to rules and evidence+

  1. Map each claim to the governing question

    60 minutes

    Rule mapping is a reasoned join among the claim, product classification, intended use, audience, medium, jurisdiction, and authority scope. Keyword overlap alone cannot establish that a rule governs.

    Open lesson →

  2. Match the claim to the evidence actually held

    65 minutes

    Evidence must fit the exact product, population, outcome, magnitude, timing, conditions, and level of certainty expressed. Related research does not automatically substantiate the advertised representation.

    Open lesson →

Assessment and evidence contract

What completion would require.

6 assessments, 5 named sources, one capstone, and a weighted rubric.

Assessments

  • Claim-type diagnostic: Classify at least 8 of 10 representations and identify the missing scope evidence for every unresolved case.
  • Review-contract knowledge check: Earn at least 80 percent and review the rationale for every response.
  • Express and implied claim extraction lab: Preserve all adjudicated express claims, identify plausible implications, and document every uncertain boundary.
  • Authority and substantiation mapping lab: Map each claim to an authority-backed rule or record a justified abstention; no unsupported approval signal is permitted.
  • Consequential escalation scenario: Name the accountable reviewer, release block, missing evidence, and permitted next step for every scenario.
  • Claims checker capstone review: Self-assess at 80 or above with no zero on authority traceability, evidence fit, escalation, or release-boundary criteria.

Capstone evidence

  • Operating contract and data boundary
  • Adjudicated claim test set
  • Dated authority and rule register
  • Evidence-fit records
  • Reviewer and escalation matrix
  • Versioned evaluation report
  • Audit and change-control record
  • Release decision and unresolved blockers

Rubric

  • Bounded scope: 15% — Defines covered product, audience, channel, jurisdiction, inputs, and non-goals.
  • Authority traceability: 20% — Every rule is dated, scoped, and linked to an authoritative source.
  • Evidence fit: 20% — Every disposition identifies the evidence held, missing, and mismatched.
  • Human governance: 20% — Names accountable reviewers, blocks, escalation, audit, and change control.
  • Failure evaluation: 15% — Reports frozen test set, denominators, false assurance, burden, and gaps.
  • Release boundary: 10% — Public language matches the tested prototype and unresolved dependencies.

Use it responsibly

What supports this—and where it stops.

Keep the practical material attached to its evidence, method, assumptions, and limits.

Evidence

  • Applicable statutes, regulations, and agency guidance
  • Product and substantiation records
  • Versioned claim-review decisions

Method

  1. Identify the exact claim and audience
  2. Resolve the applicable authority
  3. Compare wording with required evidence and qualifications
  4. Escalate ambiguity to qualified reviewers
  5. Preserve the decision record

Assumptions

  • The relevant jurisdiction and product category are defined
  • Qualified professionals review consequential findings

Limitations

  • Rules and enforcement positions change
  • The checker supports review but does not provide legal advice or final regulatory approval
