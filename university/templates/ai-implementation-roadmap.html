01What this is+

A four-phase, roughly 90-day plan for putting AI to work without the two failure modes we see most: buying a tool nobody adopts, or moving so fast that one sensitive-data mistake ends the whole effort. Each phase carries a single goal, a named owner, the guardrails that keep it safe, and a success measure — so you know whether to keep going or fix something first.

It picks up where the AI Use Case Inventory leaves off. Once you've ranked your opportunities, this is how you turn the top one into a real, adopted change — then a second, then a third.

02Who it's for+
  • Owners and operators who've decided to try AI and want a sequence to follow instead of a pile of open tabs.
  • Department leads asked to "roll out AI" who need a plan they can defend to their team and their boss.
  • Anyone who ran one good AI experiment and now has to spread it without losing the quality or the trust.
03What's inside+
  • Four phases — Foundation, Pilot, Expand, Embed — mapped across 13 weeks, each with a goal, key activities, an owner, guardrails, and a success measure.
  • A copy-pastable fill-in version of the whole roadmap, with target-date fields for every phase.
  • A short "before you start" checklist and a review-cadence note so the plan doesn't stall after week two.
  • A guardrail reminder about the two corners you should never cut to move faster.
04How to use it+
  1. Pick one use case. Take the top low-effort, low-sensitivity row from your inventory. One. Resist the urge to run three at once.
  2. Assign an owner per phase. Every phase needs one name accountable for it, not a committee. Owners can differ by phase.
  3. Set the guardrails before the work. Decide your data-handling rule and your human-review step in Phase 0, before anyone touches a tool.
  4. Move on measures, not vibes. Each phase has a success measure. Hit it before advancing; if you miss it, fix the phase you're in.
  5. Fill the dates and review weekly. Put real target dates on each phase and hold a short weekly check-in to keep it honest.

Activities: - Extend to 4-8 people (NOT the whole company) - Write a short "how we do this" guide - Run a 60-minute training session - Optionally start a 2nd use case at Phase 1 discipline - Loosen review only where proven safe; document where Owner: ____________________ Guardrails: Same data rule for all. New users keep full review until they hit the quality bar. Small group only. Success measure: Group uses it independently; quality holds; time saved scales. Target date: ____________________ ------------------------------------------------------------------ PHASE 3 · EMBED (Weeks 10-13) Goal: Make it a normal, owned part of how the team works. Activities: - Fold the guide + data rule into onboarding - Assign a standing process owner - Set a monthly quality + data-handling check - Pick the next use case (back to Phase 0) - Capture total quarterly time saved Owner: ____________________ Guardrails: Written policy, documented review standard, monthly audit. Success measure: Runs without the pilot team; named owner; next use case queued. Target date: ____________________ ------------------------------------------------------------------ REVIEW CADENCE Weekly (15 min): Are we on the target date? Any data near-misses? Blockers? End of each phase: Did we hit the success measure? Yes = advance. No = fix here first. Monthly (from Phase 3 on): Quality, data handling, tool tier still right?
Don't cut these corners to move faster: skipping the Phase 0 guardrails — the written data rule and the human-review step — is how a rollout turns into a data leak or a customer-facing mistake. And don't jump from a two-person pilot straight to the whole company; expand to a small group, prove quality holds, then widen. This is general education, not legal advice — check your own obligations before you move any sensitive data or set company policy.

Use it responsibly

What supports this—and where it stops.

Keep the practical material attached to its evidence, method, assumptions, and limits.

Evidence

  • Workflow baseline
  • Named owners and milestones
  • Pilot evaluation results
  • Approval and training records

Method

  1. Sequence adoption in phases
  2. Separate capability from readiness
  3. Assign owners
  4. Measure quality and risk
  5. Gate expansion on evidence

Assumptions

  • Leadership can assign accountable owners
  • The first pilot is bounded and reversible

Limitations

  • A generic 90-day plan must be adapted to organizational and regulatory context
  • Milestones do not guarantee successful adoption
