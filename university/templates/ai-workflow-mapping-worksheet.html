01What this is+

Most "should we use AI here?" conversations stall because nobody has written down what the work actually is. This worksheet fixes that. You take a single recurring process, lay it out step by step, and label each step with one honest decision: does AI draft it, assist with it, run it on its own, or should a person do it and only a person?

The point is not to hand the whole thing to a model. The point is to find the two or three steps where AI genuinely saves time, keep a named human on the steps that carry risk, and be able to explain both to anyone who asks.

02Who it's for+
  • Owners and operators who keep hearing "we should use AI for that" but have not decided which part of "that" is safe to automate.
  • Team leads who want to hand a repeatable process to their people with clear guardrails instead of a vague "use ChatGPT."
  • Anyone whose workflow touches customer data, money, or promises, and who needs to prove a person still checks the output.
03What's inside+
  • A worked example: one common workflow mapped end to end, so you can see what a finished map looks like.
  • A blank, copy-and-paste worksheet you can fill in for any process you run.
  • A short legend of four AI roles so you and your team use the same words for the same thing.
04How to use it+
  1. Name one workflow you do at least weekly. Write down what starts it and what "done" looks like.
  2. List every step as it happens today, before any AI. Be honest about the messy ones.
  3. For each step, pick one AI role: Draft, Assist, Automate, or Human-only. When in doubt, pick the more cautious one.
  4. For every step that touches customer data or makes a promise, write down who verifies what before it leaves.
  5. Note the risk if that step goes wrong, and how sensitive the data is. That tells you where to slow down.

Read this before you automate anything: every step that touches customer data or makes a promise on your behalf needs a named human checkpoint — a specific person who verifies a specific thing — before it goes out. The "Automate" role is only for steps that are low-risk and reversible: if it breaks, you can undo it and nobody gets hurt. Drafting a reply is not the same as sending it. This is practical guidance for reducing risk, not legal advice.

Use it responsibly

What supports this—and where it stops.

Keep the practical material attached to its evidence, method, assumptions, and limits.

Evidence

  • Observed workflow
  • Representative inputs and outputs
  • System and permission records
  • Exceptions and failure cases

Method

  1. Map the current state
  2. Separate preparation from decisions
  3. Locate irreversible actions
  4. Name accountable owners
  5. Test the map with practitioners

Assumptions

  • People who perform the work participate
  • The map is validated against real examples

Limitations

  • A diagram can omit tacit work and informal exceptions
  • Workflow suitability does not establish security, privacy, or compliance
