01What this is+

Foundations

Generative AI is software that produces new text, images, audio, or code by predicting what comes next from patterns it learned across very large amounts of example data. The systems most businesses touch are large language models (LLMs) like the ones behind ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. You give the model a prompt, and it returns a response one token at a time. Each token is chosen because it is a statistically likely continuation of everything that came before.

A useful way to hold this in your head: an LLM is a very well-read pattern completer. It has read a wide slice of the public internet, books, and documents, and it has become good at continuing text so it sounds right. That single fact explains both its strengths (fluent drafting, summarizing, reformatting, brainstorming) and its failures (confident wrong answers, invented sources, shaky arithmetic).

It is not a database you query for stored facts, and it does not check its work against the world. It is a prediction machine that is often correct because correct-sounding text and correct text overlap a lot, but not always.

02Who this is for+

Audience

This lesson is for owners, operators, and team leads who are being asked to "use AI" and want a foundation they can trust before they build anything on top of it. You do not need a technical background. If you can describe a task to a new hire in plain language, you have the skill that matters most here.

  • A small-business owner deciding whether AI belongs in customer service, marketing, or operations.
  • A manager writing a policy for how the team may and may not use these tools.
  • Anyone who has heard both "AI will replace everyone" and "AI is useless" and wants a grounded middle view.
03When to use it, and when not+

Timing

When to use it

Read this before your first serious project, before you sign up for a paid plan, and before you let AI touch anything a customer or regulator will see. This mental model is the thing that keeps you out of trouble later. Come back to it whenever a vendor makes a claim that sounds too good, because most overclaims collapse the moment you remember the model is predicting text rather than knowing facts.

When not to use it

Do not reach for generative AI as your source of truth for anything with a real-world answer you can look up: a specific law, a dosage, a tax figure, a customer's account balance, a quote you plan to attribute to a real person. The model will happily produce all of these, and it will sometimes be wrong in ways that look completely convincing. When the cost of being wrong is high and the fact is checkable, check the source, not the chatbot.

04Tools that work well+

Tools

To build the mental model, spend time in the four consumer chat tools most businesses start with. Each has a free tier that is enough to learn on.

ChatGPT

Seeing the broadest range of tasks, with strong general drafting. Free tier available.

Claude

Longer documents, careful writing, and following detailed instructions. Free tier available.

Gemini

Working inside Google Docs, Gmail, and Search. Free tier available.

Perplexity

Watching how a tool cites sources, with answers that include links you can click. Free tier available.

A good exercise: ask all four the same factual question, then check every answer against a primary source. You will learn more about how these systems behave in twenty minutes of that than in an hour of reading.

05Step-by-step workflow+

Practice

Use this to build an accurate feel for the technology.

  1. Give it a drafting task. Ask a model to write a short thank-you email to a customer. Notice how fluent and fast it is. This is the core strength.
  2. Give it a lookup task. Ask for a specific statistic or a citation, then verify it. Notice whether the source is real.
  3. Push on a wrong answer. When it gets something wrong, tell it so. Watch how it apologizes and often produces a new answer that also sounds confident. This shows you the model has no independent access to truth.
  4. Ask it to do arithmetic in its head. Try a multi-step calculation, then try again with a tool or calculator enabled. Compare.
  5. Write down your own one-sentence definition. After the exercises, describe what the tool is in your own words. That sentence is the asset you keep.
06Example prompts+

Copy these into any chat tool to feel the difference between generation and knowledge.

Write a warm, three-sentence follow-up email to a customer named Dana who just bought a kayak from our shop. Do not invent any details about the order.
I'm testing you. Give me the full citation for a peer-reviewed study showing that four-day work weeks raise productivity. Then, separately, tell me your confidence that this exact study exists and can be found by that citation.
Here is a paragraph I wrote. Rewrite it to be clearer and shorter, but do not add any facts I did not include. Flag anything you were tempted to add.
07Example outputs+

The email prompt returns something usable in seconds:

"Hi Dana, thank you so much for picking up your kayak from us today. We hope it brings you a lot of calm mornings on the water. If anything ever needs adjusting, just reach out and we'll take care of it."

The citation prompt is where the lesson lands. A well-behaved model will often say something like:

"I can describe the general finding, but I should be honest: I may not be able to give you an accurate, verifiable citation. Any specific author, journal, and year I produce should be checked against a database like Google Scholar before you rely on it. My confidence that a precise citation I generate is real and correct is low."

That second output is the whole point. The tool is useful and it is also fallible, and a good one will tell you which is which.

08Risks and common mistakes+

Watch outs

Treating fluency as accuracy

The most common error. Smooth, confident prose feels true. It is not evidence of truth.

Assuming it "knows" your business

Out of the box, the model knows nothing about your customers, prices, or policies. It fills gaps with plausible invention unless you supply the facts.

Believing the training data is current

Models have a knowledge cutoff and may not know recent events unless connected to live search. Even then, verify.

Thinking a bigger model means always right

More capable models are wrong less often, not never. The verification habit still applies.

Pasting sensitive data without checking policy

Consumer tiers may use your inputs to improve models unless you opt out or use a business plan. Know your settings before sharing anything private.

09Human review checklist+

Quality control

  • ✓ Did I ask the model to do something it is good at (generate, reshape, summarize) rather than something it cannot reliably do (recall exact facts, do precise math)?
  • ✓ Have I verified every factual claim, name, number, and citation against a primary source?
  • ✓ Did I supply the real facts about my business rather than let the model guess?
  • ✓ Is anything customer-facing, legal, medical, or financial getting a human sign-off?
  • ✓ Do I know whether my inputs are being used to train the model, and am I comfortable with that?

What supports this—and where it stops.

Keep the practical material attached to its evidence, method, assumptions, and limits.

Evidence

  • Primary technical documentation from model providers
  • Representative model outputs and failure cases
  • Current professional guidance where consequential uses are discussed

Method

  1. Define generative AI by what the model does
  2. Separate pattern generation from knowledge and judgment
  3. Compare suitable and unsuitable tasks

  5. Require verification before consequential use

Assumptions

  • Learners have no technical background

Limitations
