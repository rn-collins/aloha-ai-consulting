01What this is+

A method for turning tacit know-how, the way you actually do a recurring task, into a written standard operating procedure someone else could follow. The hard part of SOPs was never the formatting; it was pulling the steps out of your head, including the small decisions and exceptions you stopped noticing years ago. AI helps here because it can interview you, organize a brain-dump into clean steps, and ask the "what happens if" questions that surface the gaps. You supply the truth; the model supplies the structure.

02Who this is for+
  • Owners whose business runs on processes only they know, and who want to delegate or sell someday.
  • Teams onboarding new hires who keep asking the same questions because nothing is written down.
  • Anyone who has said "it would take longer to document it than to just do it," and been wrong about that for two years.
03When to use it+
  • A recurring task done more than a few times a month, especially one only one person knows.
  • Onboarding, where a written procedure saves the same explanation ten times.
  • Before someone leaves or goes on leave, so their knowledge doesn't walk out the door.
  • Cleaning up an existing SOP that's stale, vague, or nobody follows.
04When not to use it+

For processes with legal, safety, or regulatory weight, without expert sign-off

Compliance procedures, safety protocols, financial controls, or anything a regulator inspects need a qualified human to own the content. AI drafts it; the expert approves it. Nothing here is legal, medical, or financial advice.

When you can't verify the steps yourself

If you don't actually know the process, the model will fill gaps with plausible invention, and a confident wrong step in an SOP gets followed.

With confidential specifics in the wrong tool

Real credentials, client data, or trade secrets don't belong in a consumer tier. Draft with placeholders; add the sensitive details in a controlled document. See the privacy lesson.

As a one-and-done

An SOP nobody revisits rots. If you won't maintain it, a lighter checklist may serve better.

05Tools that work well+
  • A general assistant that holds the whole conversation (Claude or ChatGPT) is ideal, because drafting an SOP is really an interview. You want a tool with long context that can keep refining one document across a long back-and-forth.
  • Voice-to-text plus the assistant: talk through the process out loud, get a transcript, then paste it in. Speaking surfaces detail that typing skips.
  • Workspace-native AI (Copilot, Gemini) if you want the finished SOP to live in Word, Google Docs, or your wiki and stay editable by the team.

The real trick: ask the model to interview you rather than to "write an SOP." A blank request gets a generic template. A structured interview gets your real process, exceptions and all.

06Step-by-step workflow+
  1. Pick one narrow process. "Onboard a new client," not "run the business." Scope it to a single outcome.
  2. Brain-dump, roughly. Type or talk through how you do it; out of order is fine.
  3. Let the model interview you. Have it ask about triggers, tools, decision points, exceptions, and what "done" looks like. Answer honestly.
  4. Generate the structured draft. Purpose, when it runs, who owns it, numbered steps, decision branches, and common failure points.
  5. Test it on the harshest reader: a beginner. Could someone new follow it without you in the room? Have the model flag every step that assumes prior knowledge.
  6. Verify every step against reality. Walk through it once. Fix anything the model guessed.
  7. Get expert sign-off if stakes are high, then store it where the team will find it and set a review date.
07Example prompts+ 
I want to document an SOP for [process]. Don't write it yet. First, interview me: ask me one question at a time about what triggers this process, the tools and access needed, each step in order, the decision points, the exceptions, and how I know it's done. Keep asking until you have enough to write a procedure a new hire could follow.
Now write the SOP from my answers. Structure it as: Purpose, When to run it, Owner, Prerequisites, Numbered steps, Decision points ("if X, then Y"), Common mistakes to avoid. Use plain language. Where I didn't give you a detail, insert [NEEDS INPUT] instead of guessing.
Read this SOP as if you're a brand-new employee with no context. List every step that assumes knowledge I didn't explain, every place you'd be unsure what to do, and every term that isn't defined.
08Example output+

SOP: Onboard a New Client

Purpose: Get a signed client from contract to first deliverable with nothing dropped. Owner: Account lead. When to run: Within 1 business day of a signed agreement.

Steps

  1. Create the client folder in [system] using the naming format [NEEDS INPUT].
  2. Send the welcome email from the saved template. Personalize the first line.
  3. Schedule the kickoff call within 5 business days.
  4. Decision point: If the client is in a regulated industry, add the compliance intake step before kickoff.

Common mistakes: Skipping the folder naming format; booking kickoff too far out and losing momentum.

What to catch: the model may invent a system name, a timeline you never gave, or a step that sounds right but isn't how you work. The [NEEDS INPUT] markers are doing their job. Fill them from reality, and delete anything invented.

09Risks and common mistakes+

Plausible invention

The model fills gaps with steps that read well and are wrong. Force [NEEDS INPUT] and verify.

Skipping the interview

"Write me an SOP" yields a generic template that documents nobody's real process.

Assumed knowledge

Steps that make sense to you and baffle a new hire. Test with the beginner prompt.

No owner or review date

An SOP with no owner goes stale and gets ignored.

Shipping high-stakes procedures without expert approval

Compliance and safety SOPs need a qualified human to sign off.

10Human review checklist+
  • Every step matches how the work is actually done; I walked through it once.
  • No invented tools, timelines, or steps remain; all [NEEDS INPUT] markers are filled.
  • A true beginner could follow it without me in the room.
  • Exceptions and decision points are captured, not just the happy path.
  • High-stakes content has expert sign-off.
  • The SOP has a named owner, a home, and a review date.

Use it responsibly

What supports this—and where it stops.

Keep the practical material attached to its evidence, method, assumptions, and limits.

Evidence

  • Interviews with process owners
  • Representative inputs, outputs, exceptions, and failure cases
  • Policies and systems governing the process

Method

  1. Observe or interview the process owner
  2. Map triggers, inputs, steps, decisions, and handoffs
  3. Draft in a standard structure
  4. Test the procedure with a different user
  5. Correct omissions and unsafe assumptions
  6. Assign an owner and review date

Assumptions

  • A knowledgeable process owner validates the procedure
  • The process can be tested before broad adoption

Limitations

  • AI cannot discover tacit steps that users fail to provide
  • Regulated, safety-critical, or high-consequence procedures require qualified review and change control
