Before you begin

A hallucination is when AI states something false with complete confidence. It is built into how these systems work, so your job is to manage it, not wish it away.

This lesson is educational. Test important claims against current primary sources and use qualified review when the stakes require it.

01Confident, fluent, and wrong+

What this is

A hallucination is AI output that is fluent, confident, and false: invented facts, fake citations, made-up quotes, nonexistent people, or plausible numbers with no basis. The unsettling part is the confidence. The model shows no doubt when it invents, because it does not know it is inventing. It is doing what it always does, predicting likely text, and sometimes the most likely-sounding text is simply not true.

Why they happen

A language model generates each word by predicting what should come next from patterns, not by looking up a stored fact and confirming it. When it has seen a fact many times in training, its prediction usually matches reality. When it has not, or when your question sits in a gap, it still produces a confident, well-formed answer because that is what it was built to do. It fills the gap with something shaped like a correct answer. A fake legal citation looks exactly like a real one because the model learned the pattern of citations, not the case law. That is the mechanism, and it is why hallucinations can only be managed, never fully removed.

02Where this matters most+

Who and when

Who this is for

  • Anyone who relies on AI for facts, citations, or numbers.
  • Owners and managers writing AI-use policy and setting review requirements.
  • Professionals in high-stakes fields where a false statement carries liability.

When to use it

Keep this front of mind whenever AI output will be treated as fact: research, citations, compliance statements, medical or legal or financial content, and anything a customer, court, or regulator will read. The riskier the setting, the more this lesson applies. Share it with your team before anyone uses AI on client-facing work.

When not to worry

Purely generative tasks with no factual claim need no hallucination guard: rewording your own text, brainstorming names, drafting a tone for an email you will fact-check anyway. If there is no claim about the world, there is nothing to hallucinate. Concentrate your attention where facts live, and let AI do the creative and drafting work it handles well.

03What reduces or exposes hallucinations+

Tools

No tool eliminates hallucinations, but some lower the rate or bring inventions into the open.

Source-citing tools

Perplexity and search-enabled chat give you links to check and lower the rate of pure invention. Still open and verify each link.

Retrieval-grounded assistants

Answering only from your own documents constrains the model to supplied text, which cuts invention when the system is built well.

Confidence-flagging prompts

Ask the model to rate its own certainty and name exactly what you should check before trusting the answer.

The primary source itself

The only real fix: confirm the fact where it actually lives, in the case record, the report, or the original data.

04Step by step+

Workflow

  1. Assume any specific fact could be invented until confirmed, especially names, citations, statistics, and quotes.
  2. Prefer grounded modes. Use tools that cite sources or answer from your own documents when facts matter.
  3. Ask for sources and confidence, then treat low-confidence items as unverified by default.
  4. Check every citation twice: does it exist, and does it say what was claimed?
  5. Never submit AI-generated citations or figures to a court, client, or regulator without independent confirmation.
  6. Build a review gate so no unverified claim reaches anyone outside your team.
05Prompts that guard against invention+

Example prompts

Answer only from the document I pasted. If the document does not contain the answer, say "not in the source" rather than filling it in from general knowledge.
List every citation, statistic, name, and date in your answer. For each, tell me your confidence it is real and correct, and exactly where I should go to verify it. Flag anything you may have invented.
06What happened in Mata v. Avianca+

A real consequence

The clearest cautionary tale is Mata v. Avianca. In 2023, in the U. S. District Court for the Southern District of New York, lawyers filed a brief citing several court decisions to support their client's case. The problem: the cases did not exist. The attorneys had used ChatGPT for legal research, and the tool had hallucinated six judicial opinions, complete with case names, citations, quotations, and reasoning that read like the real thing. When opposing counsel and the court could not find the cases, the lawyers asked ChatGPT to confirm them, and the tool insisted they were real and could be found on Westlaw and LexisNexis. They were not.

Judge P. Kevin Castel sanctioned the attorneys under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 11, imposing a $5,000 penalty and requiring them to notify each judge who had been falsely named as the author of a fabricated opinion. The case became a global warning and is now routinely cited when AI-invented case law appears in court. In 2024 the American Bar Association issued its first formal ethics opinion on lawyers' use of generative AI.

The takeaway is not that AI is dangerous and should be avoided. The lawyers skipped the one step that would have caught everything: opening a legal database and confirming the cases existed. The tool did what tools do. The humans did not verify.

07Risks and common mistakes+

Pitfalls

Submitting AI citations without checking

The Mata mistake. Fake citations look identical to real ones, so a quick glance never catches them. Open the source every time.

Asking the model to confirm itself

It will often double down on the invention with more confidence. Confirmation has to come from outside the model.

Assuming newer or bigger models do not hallucinate

They hallucinate less, not never. The verification habit still applies at every model size.

Trusting a cited link without opening it

Models sometimes attach a real URL to a claim the page does not actually make. Read the page, not just the link.

No review gate

Without a required check before publication, one confident invention can reach a customer or a court.

08Human review before anything ships+

Checklist

  • Did I confirm every citation both exists and supports the claim?
  • Did I verify every name, statistic, quote, and date against a primary source?
  • Did I avoid using the same model to confirm its own claims?
  • For grounded tasks, did the answer stay inside the supplied documents?
  • Is there a required review gate before anything factual reaches an outside party?
  • For legal, medical, or financial content, did a qualified professional sign off?

What supports this—and where it stops.

Keep the practical material attached to its evidence, method, assumptions, and limits.

Evidence

  • Primary court and professional records for documented failures
  • Provider documentation on model limitations
  • Known-good and known-bad test cases

Method

  1. Identify claims that require authority
  2. Require source-level evidence
  3. Separate fluent wording from factual support
  4. Test citations and quotations directly
  5. Escalate unresolved claims before publication

Assumptions

  • Users have access to authoritative sources
  • Consequential output receives accountable human review

Limitations

  • No prompting technique eliminates hallucinations
  • Risk varies with task, model, retrieval, and review quality
