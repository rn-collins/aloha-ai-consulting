01What this is+

Foundations

This is a practical taxonomy of business work, sorted by how well current generative AI performs it. The dividing line is not "hard versus easy" the way a person would judge it. It is "does this task reward fluent pattern completion, or does it require verified truth, live data, precise math, or accountability?"

AI is strong when the task is to produce a draft, reshape existing information, or generate options. It is weak when the task is to be the authoritative source of a fact, to act on real-time private data it has not been given, or to make a judgment that someone must answer for. The rest of this lesson turns that principle into a checklist you can apply to any request that lands on your desk.

The one-line test: if a smart, fast intern could draft it from information you hand them, and you would still review it, AI can probably help. If the task needs a guaranteed-correct fact, a live system lookup, or a signature, AI is a starting point at most.

02Who this is for and when to use it+

Audience

  • Owners and operators deciding where to pilot AI first.
  • Team leads triaging a backlog of "can AI do this?" requests.
  • Anyone building a business case and needing a sober view of the payoff.

You do not need to know how the models work. You do need to know your own workflows well enough to describe them.

Use this map when you are choosing a first project, when a vendor pitches an "AI solution" and you want to sanity-check it, and when you are writing the rules for how your team may use these tools. Start where the task is high-volume, low-stakes, and easy to review. That is where AI pays off fastest and where a mistake costs the least while you learn.

03When not to use it+

Boundaries

Avoid AI as the decision-maker or the final word in these situations:

  • Anything where a wrong answer causes real harm and cannot be caught in review: dosing, safety instructions, structural or financial calculations people act on directly.
  • Authoritative facts about your own operations that the model has no access to: account balances, order status, inventory, who owes what. Connect a real system for those; do not ask the model to guess.
  • Legal, medical, and financial advice presented as reliable. AI can help you prepare questions for a professional. It is not the professional.
  • Situations demanding accountability. When someone must be answerable for a decision, a human makes it. The model cannot be held responsible.
04Tools that work well+

Fit

First drafts

Marketing copy, emails, job posts. Reach for ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini.

Summarizing long documents and meetings

Claude handles long docs well; Gemini works inside Google apps.

Answering with cited web sources

Perplexity, or the search modes in ChatGPT and Gemini.

Reformatting data, drafting spreadsheets and code

ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini. Always run and check what they produce.

Customer answers from your knowledge base

Use a retrieval-based assistant grounded in your own docs, not raw chat.

Exact math, live account data, legal rulings

Use connected tools, databases, and professionals for these.

05Step-by-step workflow+

Method

  1. List the task in plain words. Write what a person would actually do, start to finish.
  2. Sort each step into generate/reshape (AI-friendly) or verify/decide/lookup (human or connected system).
  3. Score the stakes. If it is wrong, who notices, how fast, and how bad is it? Low-stakes and reviewable goes first.
  4. Check the data. Does AI need facts it does not have? Either give it those facts in the prompt or connect a real source.
  5. Design the review step before you launch. Decide who checks output and against what.
  6. Pilot small, measure, then scale only the steps that saved real time without adding risk.
06Example prompts+

Prompts

Here are my last five approved customer replies (pasted below). Draft a reply to this new message in the same tone. Only use facts from the message and my notes. If you need information I didn't give you, list your questions instead of guessing.
Summarize this 12-page vendor contract into a one-page brief for a non-lawyer: key dates, payment terms, termination, and anything unusual. Quote the exact clause for each point so I can verify it. Flag anything you're unsure about.
I run a landscaping company. Draft three social posts about our spring cleanup service. Do not invent prices, dates, or promotions. Leave a [bracket] anywhere I need to fill in a real detail.
07Example outputs+

Results

The contract prompt returns a brief that is genuinely useful because it quotes the source:

Payment terms: Net 30 from invoice date. Clause 4.2: "Payment shall be due within thirty (30) days of receipt of a valid invoice." Unusual: an auto-renewal with a 90-day cancellation window. Clause 9.1. This is longer than typical; confirm you can meet it. I am not certain this is enforceable in your state, so treat it as a flag for a lawyer, not advice.

The landscaping prompt returns posts with honest gaps left for you:

"Spring is here and so is the mess winter left behind. Book your spring cleanup with us and get your yard ready for the season. [Add your offer or start date here.] Call [phone] to reserve a slot."
08Risks and common mistakes+

Pitfalls

Aiming AI at a lookup problem

"What's this customer's balance?" is a database question, not a chat question. The model will guess.

Skipping the review step to save time

The review is where the value gets protected. Cutting it turns a time-saver into a liability.

Piloting on your highest-stakes work

Learn on the low-risk tasks first, where a caught error is cheap.

Believing vendor claims without a test

Ask to see the tool fail. One that cannot show you its limits is hiding them.

Grounding a customer bot in the open model instead of your own documents

Ungrounded, it will invent policies you do not have.

09Human review checklist+

Quality gate

  • ✓ Is this a generate/reshape task, or does it secretly need a verified fact or live data?
  • ✓ Did I supply the real information, or is the model filling gaps by inventing?
  • ✓ Are the stakes low enough that a caught error is cheap, or do I need tighter controls?
  • ✓ Is there a named human reviewing anything customer-facing or high-stakes?
  • ✓ For legal, medical, or financial content, is a qualified professional in the loop?

Use it responsibly

What supports this—and where it stops.

Keep the practical material attached to its evidence, method, assumptions, and limits.

Evidence

  • Representative workflow inputs and outputs
  • Documented model limitations
  • Business requirements and consequence levels

Method

  1. Describe the task before selecting a tool
  2. Separate drafting and classification from authority and judgment
  3. Score reversibility and consequence
  4. Run a representative test
  5. Assign human review before adoption

Assumptions

  • A workflow owner can define acceptable output
  • The organization can identify consequential decisions

Limitations

  • Performance varies by model, data, and task
  • Task suitability does not establish privacy, security, or regulatory acceptability
